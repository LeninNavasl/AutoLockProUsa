Modern vehicles equipped with push-button ignition and keyless entry rely on sophisticated two-way wireless communication between a smart proximity fob and interior antennas scattered across the cabin and trunk.

When you purchase a replacement fob from an aftermarket retailer or online catalog, it will not operate your car until it undergoes specialized diagnostic programming. Here is how modern mobile locksmiths complete this pairing process on-site.

## The Two Layers of Smart Key Security

Every proximity smart key combines two distinct wireless protocols:

1. **Passive Radio Frequency Identification (RFID):** An unpowered microchip inside the fob head that operates at 125 kHz or 134.2 kHz. Even if your fob battery is completely drained, holding the fob against the start button allows the vehicle's inductive exciter coil to read this chip and crank the engine.
2. **Active Ultra-High Frequency (UHF) Transceiver:** Operating on 315 MHz (North America) or 433.92 MHz (Europe/Asia), this active transmitter unlocks the doors as you approach the vehicle and authenticates your presence inside the driver seat.

## The On-Site Diagnostic Pairing Process

Unlike older mechanical keys that could sometimes be programmed using manual "pedal-and-ignition cycles", modern smart fobs require direct communication with the vehicle's CAN-bus network:

- **OBD-II Connection:** The mobile locksmith plugs a commercial automotive key programmer into the 16-pin diagnostic port beneath the dashboard.
- **PIN/Security Bypass:** Many manufacturers (including Ford, Chrysler/Dodge/Jeep, Nissan, and General Motors) require a dynamic security rolling code (PIN). The diagnostic tool calculates or bypasses this code securely.
- **Key Learn Procedure:** The ECU enters programming mode. The technician introduces the new OEM-specification smart key to the center console antenna, completing synchronization in under 15 minutes.
